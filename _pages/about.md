I am a PhD student in the [Cryptology and Data Security Group](https://crypto.unibe.ch/) at the University of Bern, working on the privacy and scalability of digital currencies. This covers both decentralized cryptocurrencies and centrally managed systems like CBDCs.

A first step is developing formal definitions for privacy: how do we precisely characterize and compare what different systems actually guarantee? Once we have those definitions, the next step is building cryptosystems that can provably meet them or bring new privacy properties to the table. On the scalability side, I am particularly interested in the storage overhead that privacy-preserving mechanisms introduce and what that means for node synchronization. More broadly, I work on privacy-enhancing technologies and censorship-resistant systems, drawing on cryptography and Byzantine fault-tolerant protocols.

A recurring theme across all of this is the tension between privacy and regulatory oversight. It is a tension that is not unique to digital currencies; it exists just as much in the traditional financial system. I find it exciting to design mechanisms where people have real privacy and regulators can still act when it matters. And beyond just satisfying compliance requirements, cryptography gives us something more powerful: the ability to enforce rules at a technical level, providing guarantees against regime change or corruption that law alone cannot offer.
